The Computation Graph of an LSTM

There is no in-person tutorial this week. Instead, please work through this sheet on your own; it should take about 50 minutes. Computation graphs and RNNs are both typical exam topics, so it is well worth the time.

Guiding question: why can an LSTM carry information across many more time steps than a simple RNN? You will draw the complete computation graph of one LSTM step by hand and then see, with actual numbers, where the gradient vanishes and where it does not.

What you need

  • Pen and paper (or any drawing tool you like) and a calculator.
  • Simon's autodiff visualizer, which you know from the lecture. Every Open ↗ link below opens it with an expression and values already filled in. The visualizer computes with concrete numbers, so each part gives you values for the inputs and the weights. These values are invented for the exercise; in a real network, the weights would be learned. The Visual Diagram tab shows the graph with the forward value and the gradient (the overlined value) in each node, and the Step-by-Step Walkthrough tab lists every local derivative. You can change the values and run it again.

Please draw each graph first and only then open the link: comparing your own drawing with the result is where most of the learning happens.

To keep the graphs readable, we use a single hidden unit, so all vectors and matrices become single numbers. The structure of the graph stays the same as with vectors. We also leave out the bias terms.

Part 1: One step of a simple RNN 10 min

ht = tanh(xt Wxh + ht−1 Whh)

Values: xt = 1, ht−1 = 0.1, Wxh = 0.5, Whh = 0.5.

  1. Draw the computation graph. The leaf nodes are the inputs xt and ht−1 and the weights Wxh and Whh.
  2. Forward pass: write the values into the leaves and compute the value of every other node, up to ht.
  3. Backward pass: start with the gradient 1 at ht and compute the gradient of ht with respect to every other node, down to the four leaves. Hint: the derivative of tanh(a) is 1 − tanh2(a). The visualizer writes it as cosh(a)**(-2), that is 1 / cosh2(a), which is the same function.
  4. ❓ On its way from ht back to ht−1, the gradient is multiplied by two local derivatives. Which ones?
  5. Check your graph and your numbers in the visualizer, where the variables are typed as x, h, wxh and whh. One RNN step ↗

Part 2: The full LSTM step by hand 20 min

These are the LSTM formulas from the lecture, without the bias terms; c̃t is the candidate for the new cell state:

ft = σ(xt Wxf + ht−1 Whf) it = σ(xt Wxi + ht−1 Whi)
c̃t = tanh(xt Wxc + ht−1 Whc) ot = σ(xt Wxo + ht−1 Who)
ct = ft ⊙ ct−1 + it ⊙ c̃t ht = ot ⊙ tanh(ct)

Values: xt = 1, ht−1 = 0.5, ct−1 = 0.3, and the weights below (typed as wxf, whf and so on in the visualizer).

Wxf = 1Whf = 0.6 Wxi = 0.4Whi = −0.3 Wxc = 0.8Whc = 0.5 Wxo = 0.3Who = 0.5

Draw the complete computation graph (forward pass only, no gradients) of one step, and label the operator nodes with the names from the formulas (ft, it, c̃t, ot, ct, ht).

Some help:

  • The leaf nodes are the inputs xt, ht−1 and ct−1 and the eight weights.
  • xt and ht−1 each feed into all four branches: the three gates and the candidate c̃t.
  • Start with one gate, for example ot, and check it in the visualizer. The other three branches follow the same pattern. Output gate ↗

Then compare your drawing with the complete step. Full LSTM step ↗ The visualizer numbers the intermediate nodes n1, n2, …, and each node shows how it is computed from the previous ones, for example n3 = n1 + n2. So first find out which of them are ft, it, c̃t, ot and ct. The easiest way is to start from the weights, whose names contain the gate: following the edges from wxf and whf upwards, the σ node you reach is ft. To check, compute ft by hand and compare it with the value of that node.

To focus on the cell state, the gates and the candidate are fixed numbers here: f = 0.9, i = 0.5 and c̃ = 0.5, the same at every step, so we drop the index t. The cell starts at c0 = 0.1. In the visualizer, c̃ is typed as ctilde. Read off the gradient at c0. Then set f to 1 and to 0.5. Five cell state steps ↗
  4. ❓ What does an LSTM have to learn in order to keep a piece of information for many steps? And what happens when ft is close to 0?

Part 4: RNN and LSTM over twenty steps 10 min

Finally, let us compare the two over many steps. The chart shows the gradient that reaches h0 when the RNN from Part 1 runs for twenty steps, and the gradient that reaches c0 along the cell state from Part 3. The RNN uses the values from Part 1 and receives the same input x = 1 at every step. Note that the vertical axis is logarithmic: every grid line is a factor of 1,000.

RNN: gradient at h0LSTM: gradient at c0

The two sliders are independent and do not need to have the same value, since they are different kinds of numbers: whh is a weight of the RNN, while f is the value of the LSTM's forget gate, which always lies between 0 and 1. What you compare is how much of the gradient survives each step back. In the RNN, it is multiplied by Whh and by the derivative of tanh; along the cell state, it is multiplied only by f.

  1. With the default values, compare the two gradients after one, five and twenty steps. If you hover over the chart, it shows the exact numbers.
  2. Move the whh slider and try to make the RNN gradient after twenty steps as large as possible. ❓ Why does it remain tiny, however you set whh?
    Hint

    Look at the two factors separately. What happens to their product when Whh is small? And what happens to tanh and its derivative when Whh is large?

  3. Now move the f slider. ❓ How close to 1 does f have to be so that, after twenty steps, the gradient is still at least 0.1? What happens when f is small?
  4. ❓ What does this mean for a model that has to connect a word with another word twenty positions earlier?

In a real LSTM, the gates themselves depend on ht−1, so there are further paths through the gates, and the gradient along those can vanish just as in the RNN. The direct path through the cell state, however, remains.

Check your answers

Part 1

Forward pass: xt Wxh = 0.5 and ht−1 Whh = 0.05, so the sum is 0.55 and ht = tanh(0.55) ≈ 0.50.

Backward pass: the gradient at the input of tanh is 1 − ht2 = 1 − 0.502 ≈ 0.75. The addition passes it on unchanged to both products, and each multiplication passes it on multiplied by the other factor. The gradients at the leaves are therefore 0.75 · Wxh ≈ 0.37 at xt, 0.75 · xt = 0.75 at Wxh, 0.75 · Whh ≈ 0.37 at ht−1 and 0.75 · ht−1 ≈ 0.07 at Whh.

On the way to ht−1, the gradient is thus multiplied by the derivative of tanh (0.75) and by the weight Whh = 0.5, which gives about 0.37.

The visualizer shows the same 0.75 as cosh(n3)**(-2): since cosh2 − sinh2 = 1, dividing by cosh2 gives 1 − tanh2 = 1 / cosh2.

Part 2

The forget gate is ft = σ(1 · 1 + 0.5 · 0.6) = σ(1.3) ≈ 0.79. In the full step, ft = 0.79, it = 0.56, c̃t = 0.78, ct = 0.68, ot = 0.63 and ht = 0.37. In the visualizer, these are the nodes n8 (ft), n13 (it), n17 (c̃t), n19 (ct), n4 (ot) and out (ht). Your drawing should contain four branches of the same shape (two multiplications by weights, an addition and a σ or tanh), all fed by the same xt and ht−1. On top of them, ct is the sum of two elementwise products, and ht is the product of ot and tanh(ct).

Last year's solution slide (the last page) shows the same graph for vectors, in last year's notation (j instead of t, z instead of c̃t and Wxf instead of Wxf); you can ignore the state st at the end.

Part 3

On the path from ct−1 to ct there is only one elementwise multiplication by ft and one addition: no tanh and no weight. Therefore, the gradient at c equals the value of ft (0.79): in ct = ft · ct−1 + it · c̃t, only the first term depends on ct−1.

Over five steps, the gradient becomes f5: 0.59 for f = 0.9. With f = 1, the gradient passes through unchanged, and with f = 0.5 it drops to 0.03. An LSTM therefore has to learn to keep ft close to 1 for information it wants to remember, while the input gate it decides what is written into the cell. A forget gate close to 0 deliberately erases the memory, which can be useful, for example, at the start of a new sentence.

Part 4

After one step, the RNN gradient is 0.37, as in Part 1. After five steps, it is only 2.14e-3, and after twenty steps 4.37e-12, whereas the gradient along the cell state is still 0.59 and 0.12 (that is, 0.95 and 0.920).

At every step, the RNN gradient is multiplied by Whh and by the derivative of tanh. This derivative is at most 1. With small weights, the product is small anyway; with large weights, the tanh saturates: the hidden state quickly gets close to 1 (about 0.99 for whh=2), so the derivative of tanh is close to 0. Either way, the gradient shrinks with every step: the largest value after twenty steps that you can reach is about 1.57e-11, at whh=0.7.

For a simple RNN, this means that a word twenty positions earlier has almost no influence on the weight updates, and the model can hardly learn such long-range dependencies. If Whh times the derivative of tanh stays above 1, the gradient can explode instead.

Along the cell state, f has to be at least 0.9, since 0.920 ≈ 0.12 but 0.8920 ≈ 0.097. With a small f, the cell state loses the gradient just as quickly as the RNN: for f = 0.27, only 4.24e-12 is left after twenty steps. The difference is that the LSTM learns its gates, so it can keep information when it is needed and erase it when it is not.
